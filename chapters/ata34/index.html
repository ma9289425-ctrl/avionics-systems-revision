01Radio NavigationVOR, ADF, DME and RNAV⌄

Start with the question each system answers: VOR gives course/radial information, ADF gives the direction of a beacon, DME gives distance, and RNAV creates a usable route between waypoints.

VOR — course and radial

  • A ground VOR transmits 360 radials. A radial identifies a line extending from the station.
  • The OBS selects the desired course; the CDI shows lateral deviation; the flag indicates TO or FROM.
  • An airport VOR may be called a TVOR. A combined VOR and TACAN installation is a VORTAC.
  • VOT check: tune 108.0 MHz, centre the CDI and expect 0° FROM or 180° TO. Error must not exceed ±4°.

ADF / NDB — direction to a beacon

  • The receiver is tuned to an NDB, and the indication shows the direction of the station.
  • The loop antenna senses direction; the sense antenna removes the 180° ambiguity.
  • ADF information may appear on a dedicated indicator or an RMI.
  • Correct antenna alignment with the aircraft longitudinal axis is important for accurate indication.

DME — distance

  • DME operates in the 962–1213 MHz range and calculates distance from the travel time of interrogation and reply pulses.
  • The display is normally slant distance, not horizontal ground distance.
  • When the aircraft is high and close to the station, the displayed distance can be noticeably greater than the ground distance.
  • DME HOLD keeps the selected DME station while the VOR frequency is changed.

RNAV — waypoint navigation

  • RNAV allows point-to-point navigation without flying directly over every ground station.
  • A waypoint may be defined by latitude/longitude or by a VOR radial plus distance.
  • VOR-based RNAV can create a calculated “phantom” waypoint.
  • The supporting VOR stations still need to be within usable line-of-sight range.
Memory line: VOR = course/radial · ADF = direction to station · DME = distance · RNAV = computed waypoint route.
02ILS & Flight GuidanceLocalizer, glideslope, markers and flight director⌄

ILS provides two kinds of approach guidance: the localizer keeps the aircraft aligned with the runway centreline, while the glideslope keeps it on the required descent path.

ElementMain purposeKey information
LocalizerLateral guidance to the runway centreline108.10–111.95 MHz; 90/150 Hz modulation
GlideslopeVertical guidance to the touchdown area329.3–335.0 MHz; approximately 3°; antenna about 1,000 ft from the threshold
Outer markerPosition indication on final approach75 MHz carrier; 400 Hz audio; blue light
Middle markerPosition near the decision-height region75 MHz carrier; 1,300 Hz audio; amber light; about 3,500 ft from the runway
Inner markerPosition close to the threshold / decision region75 MHz carrier; 3,000 Hz audio; white light

Localizer correction rule

150 Hz stronger: CDI moves left, so correct left. 90 Hz stronger: CDI moves right, so correct right.

The indication tells the pilot which side of the runway centreline the aircraft is on.

Flight director vs autopilot

The flight director calculates and displays the required attitude using command bars. The pilot follows those commands.

An autopilot goes further by moving the flight controls automatically through servos.

Memory line: Localizer = left/right · Glideslope = up/down · Marker beacons = position along the approach.
03FMS, GPS, WAAS & IRSManagement, satellite position and inertial reference⌄

These systems work together: GPS and IRS provide position information, while the FMS organises the route, calculations, radio tuning and display of navigation data.

FMS, FMC and CDU

  • The FMC is the main flight-management computer; the CDU is the pilot interface.
  • The FMS exchanges information with other aircraft systems through data buses.
  • It can store flight plans, calculate navigation information and automatically tune radio-navigation frequencies as the route progresses.
  • Built-In Test Equipment (BITE) monitors operation and records faults for maintenance.

GPS

  • GPS calculates range from signal travel time and the known position of each satellite.
  • Three satellites provide a two-dimensional latitude/longitude fix.
  • A fourth satellite adds altitude, producing a three-dimensional fix.
  • The book describes 24 satellites: 21 active and 3 spares in six orbits.

WAAS

  • Approximately 25 surveyed ground stations monitor GPS signals and calculate corrections.
  • The corrections are sent through geostationary communication satellites to a WAAS-enabled receiver.
  • The book gives approximately 1–3 m lateral and vertical position error.
  • WAAS can support precision approach capability without conventional approach equipment at every airport.

INS / IRS

  • The starting position is entered while the aircraft is stationary during initialization.
  • Position is then calculated from measured acceleration; error can gradually accumulate with time.
  • Modern IRS equipment uses three ring-laser gyros and three solid-state accelerometers.
  • GPS may initialize or correct the IRS, while the inertial system can continue if GPS is unavailable.
GPS / IRS position→ FMC calculations→ CDU and flight displays→ Pilot / autoflight guidance
Memory line: GPS measures satellite ranges · IRS measures motion · FMS combines and manages the navigation solution.
04Surveillance & Collision AvoidanceTransponder, TCAS and ADS-B⌄

Keep the roles separate: the transponder replies to surveillance interrogation, TCAS evaluates nearby traffic, and ADS-B continuously shares or receives aircraft data.

ATC transponder

  • Ground interrogation is transmitted on 1030 MHz; the aircraft reply is transmitted on 1090 MHz.
  • A conventional unit provides 4,096 selectable codes. IDENT makes the target more noticeable on the controller's display.
  • ALT / Mode C transmits pressure altitude supplied by an altitude encoder, normally in 100 ft increments.
  • Mode S assigns a unique aircraft identity and permits selective interrogation.

TCAS

  • A Traffic Advisory (TA) warns the crew and helps them locate the traffic.
  • A Resolution Advisory (RA) gives a specific vertical avoidance instruction.
  • Two TCAS II aircraft can coordinate opposite vertical actions so their commands do not conflict.
  • TCAS uses transponder interrogation and reply information to assess the threat.

ADS-B OUT

Combines GPS-derived position with information such as altitude, velocity and time, then broadcasts it to suitably equipped aircraft and ground stations.

It is automatic and does not wait for a conventional radar interrogation before broadcasting.

ADS-B IN

Receives traffic or ground-provided information and presents it to the flight crew.

Accurate testing is essential because traffic awareness and separation depend on correct position and status data.

Memory line: Transponder = reply · TCAS = assess and advise · ADS-B OUT = transmit own data · ADS-B IN = receive traffic data.
05Weather Detection & Radio AltimeterRadar, lightning, uplinked weather and AGL height⌄

Weather systems do not all sense the same thing: radar detects precipitation returns, lightning equipment detects electrical activity, and uplink services deliver remotely collected weather information.

Onboard weather radar

  • The antenna transmits short SHF pulses and listens for energy reflected by precipitation.
  • Stronger precipitation generally gives a stronger return: green = light, yellow = medium, red = heavy and magenta = intense/extreme.
  • Clouds may produce no useful return, and conventional radar does not detect dry-air turbulence.
  • Ground returns and terrain clutter must be distinguished from weather.

Radar safety

  • Do not look into a transmitting radar antenna.
  • Follow approved procedures for hangars, buildings, refuelling areas and radome materials.
  • Maintenance and operation must follow the equipment manufacturer's instructions and be performed by qualified personnel.

Lightning and uplinked weather

  • A loop antenna gives lightning-strike azimuth; signal intensity is used to estimate range.
  • The book describes dedicated lightning displays with a range of approximately 200 miles.
  • Weather reports and radar imagery may also be sent from ground or satellite services for a wider-area picture.

Radio altimeter

  • Measures height above the terrain directly below the aircraft rather than pressure altitude.
  • It is mainly used below approximately 2,500 ft, especially during approaches and low-level flight.
  • A selected decision height can trigger visual or aural warning.
  • The information may also be used by ground-proximity warning systems.
Memory line: Radar = precipitation · Lightning detector = electrical activity · Uplink = remote weather picture · Radio altimeter = true height above terrain.
06ACARSAircraft-ground digital data link⌄

ACARS is a messaging system, not a navigation sensor. It moves operational, traffic-control and administrative information between the aircraft and ground organisations.

Purpose and communication path

  • ACARS exchanges digital messages between aircraft and ground stations.
  • Early systems mainly used VHF; newer installations may also use SATCOM.
  • The system can send information automatically or allow the crew to exchange messages.

CMU

The Communications Management Unit acts as the data router. It receives information from aircraft systems and directs it through the available air-ground communication link.

Where several links are available, the CMU may select the most suitable one.

Three message groups

  • Air Traffic Control
  • Aeronautical Operational Control
  • Airline Administrative Control

Typical examples

  • Updated arrival information can be sent during descent.
  • A touchdown or movement report can be transmitted automatically.
  • Maintenance information may be sent before arrival so technicians can prepare.
Aircraft systems / crew→ CMU→ VHF or SATCOM→ ATC / operations / airline
Memory line: ACARS carries the message; the CMU routes it.
07Avionics Installation & TestingMounting, bonding, BITE and test equipment⌄

Reliable avionics depend on both correct installation and correct testing. A serviceable unit can still perform badly if mounting, cooling, bonding, wiring or calibration is wrong.

Installation essentials

  • Use secure mounting and adequate clearance; protect equipment from excess heat and moisture.
  • Shock mounts isolate vibration and landing loads.
  • A mount that is too stiff transmits shock; one that is too soft allows excessive movement.
  • Because shock-mount materials may be electrical insulators, a bonding jumper may be required to ground the equipment to aircraft structure.

BITE

  • Monitors signals and operating parameters.
  • Detects and helps isolate faults.
  • Stores, displays, prints or transmits maintenance information.
  • It supports troubleshooting but does not replace all external testing, especially when faults involve several interacting systems.
Test equipmentMain use
MultimeterMeasures voltage, current and resistance; advanced units may also measure frequency, capacitance or temperature.
OscilloscopeDisplays voltage against time so waveforms, spikes and signal behaviour can be examined.
TDRFinds opens or shorts in a transmission line and indicates the distance to the fault.
Pitot-static testerApplies controlled pressure for testing pitot-static instruments within aircraft limits.
Data-bus analyserMonitors, records and may transmit data to troubleshoot communication between avionics units.

Testing and calibration rule

Follow the aircraft and equipment manufacturer's procedures. Test equipment must be calibrated, and its accuracy must be equal to or better than the accuracy required for the equipment being tested.

Memory line: Install correctly · bond correctly · test with the correct procedure · trust only calibrated equipment.
